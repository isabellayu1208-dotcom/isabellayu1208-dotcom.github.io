Accepted · EDUAIT 2026 App · Patent

FairPlay Lab — equitable playing time in school soccer

An Auditable Mobile Decision-Support System for Equitable Playing Time and Team Cohesion in School Soccer · Ying Yee (Isabella) Yu, Kyler Harris · SoftCom Lab, Cal Poly Pomona

FairPlay Lab is a cross-platform app that turns records a volunteer coach can keep — training attendance, a seven-day RPE average, match minutes, and tallied pitch events — into three inspectable indicators: a squad fairness index, a hero-ball versus team-ball cohesion split, and a per-athlete academic crash-risk band. Every substitution suggestion opens an itemized explanation card. The scoring is rule-based, not learned.

Accepted at the 4th International Conference on Education, AI & Information Technology (EDUAIT 2026, Toronto). A model audit of the shipped rotation engine showed that RPE produced an 8-minute swing versus 6 for minute history and 4 for attendance, confined every recommendation to a 24–42 minute window in an 80-minute match, and exposed a high-load threshold discontinuity that inverted the intended penalty. The paper reports no field study; the audit is of the decision surfaces, not of coaches using the app in games. Live on the App Store; U.S. provisional patent 64/164,480 (29 Sep 2026).

Under review · Critical Debates

AI detector bias on ELL school essays

False Positives on Free and Open AI Writing Detectors: A Replication–Extension Study of English Language Learner Essays in School Writing Corpora · Ying Yee (Isabella) Yu · mentored by Dr. Mani, Carnegie Mellon University

Liang et al. (2023) found high false-positive rates when GPT detectors scored non-native TOEFL essays. I tested whether the same ELL disadvantage appears on free and open detectors applied to school writing (ASAP 2.0 / ELLIPSE), using ZeroGPT and an open RoBERTa classifier on two samples of 100 ELL and 100 native human-written essays each.

Contrary to my hypothesis, ELL essays were not flagged more often (ZeroGPT 1% vs 1% and 5% vs 0%; RoBERTa 26% vs 20% and 14% vs 9%; all p > .05). False positives tracked shorter, less lexically diverse text rather than ELL status. Schools should not treat a single free detector score as evidence of misconduct.

Limitation: each confirmatory sample is 200 essays. That is enough to reject a large ELL gap of the size reported on TOEFL text, but it is underpowered for small differences and for detector versions that change after the August 2026 collection window.

UChicago Summer Immersive · Final project

The effect of school choice on participants

Quantitative Evaluation for Public Policy · July 2026 · Irene Gong, Kristen Gumagay, Ying Yee (Isabella) Yu · presenting Cullen, Jacob & Levitt (2006)

Group 9’s final project walks through the causal design in Cullen, Jacob, and Levitt’s Econometrica study of Chicago Public Schools lotteries. Winning a seat is random, so differences between winners and losers identify an intent-to-treat effect; using the lottery as an instrument identifies LATE for students who actually enroll.

Across 194 lotteries and 14,434 students, winners attended objectively better schools, but test scores, graduation, attendance, and credits barely moved. Behavioral outcomes did: fewer disciplinary incidents and lower arrest and incarceration rates.

The exercise trained me to separate ITT from LATE, to name the identifying assumptions, and to treat a null academic result as a finding rather than a failure of the design.

In progress · Senior project

School resources, not school type

Basis Independent Brooklyn senior research · 2026–27

The research question is whether gaps in academic and college outcomes between New York City public and private high schools are explained by measurable school resources — staffing, course offerings, spending, and student composition — rather than by the public/private label itself.

It builds on my UChicago summer project on Cullen, Jacob, and Levitt (2006). That paper uses lotteries to identify the effect of being offered a seat at a higher-rated school; winners attended better schools, but test scores and graduation barely moved. The senior project asks a related question the lottery design does not: when we cannot randomize sector, how much of the public–private gap is a resource gap we can name.

Data
Planned school-level sources: NYC DOE School Quality Reports and NYSED report cards for public schools; NCES Private School Universe Survey and IRS Form 990 filings where available; College Scorecard / IPEDS for postsecondary outcomes. No student-level identifiable data. Collection has not started.
Methods
Observational school-level comparison: describe the raw public–private gap, then ask how much of it remains after conditioning on resource and composition measures (regression and matching). This will not recover a lottery-style causal effect; the aim is to test whether “sector” is a proxy.
Timeline
Fall 2026: literature and data inventory. Winter 2026–27: assemble the school-level file. Spring 2027: analysis and write-up. Now at the design stage.

About

The through-line is fairness plus data. I pick a claim that people treat as obvious — who deserves minutes, whose essay looks “AI,” whether “private” explains better outcomes — and ask whether the numbers support it. Sport is one field for that habit, not the whole map. School detectors and school resources are the same question in a different building.

I am a senior at Basis Independent Brooklyn. I play varsity soccer as a defender, and watching minutes get decided from memory is where FairPlay Lab started: load, rest, and schoolwork pull in opposite directions, and I wanted a system a volunteer coach could actually use — and audit.

The same habit — test a claim, then report what the data actually said — runs through the education work. An AI-detector study I expected to confirm bias against English learners did not; errors tracked textual features instead. A UChicago summer project on Chicago school-choice lotteries showed that winning a seat at a better school barely moved test scores. My senior project asks the next question: when we cannot randomize sector, how much of New York City’s public–private gap is a resource gap we can name.

I am applying to undergraduate programs in sports analytics, statistics, and quantitative social science.
